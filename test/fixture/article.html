The lost frontier of stretchtext

In 1967 Ted Nelson sketched text that grows where a reader wants more and shrinks back where they don't. It took cheap writing to make the idea usable.

By the StretchText desk 6 min read

Ted Nelson proposed stretchtext in 1967 as a way to keep a document at whatever depth a reader needed. Instead of choosing one length for everyone, the text would offer a short version in place, with the removed material still attached to the phrases it came from. A reader who wanted the full account could pull it back, sentence by sentence, without leaving the paragraph or loading a second page.

The idea failed for a practical reason rather than a conceptual one. Every level of detail had to be written and maintained by hand, and a document with three depths of detail is three documents pretending to be one. Authors never had the patience for it, publishers never had the budget, and hypertext systems that supported the feature could not show a return on the work.

Generated text changes the arithmetic. A model can compress a passage to a sixth of its length in under a second, and it can produce the missing detail again on demand, at the precise point where a reader asks for it. The author writes once. The reader chooses a depth by clicking, and the depth is not a setting somewhere in a menu but a gesture on the sentence itself.

The economics are the interesting part. Compression is not free, but it is small: a few hundred tokens for a long paragraph, billed to whoever asked for it. That makes a different bargain possible, in which the reader pays for their own reading instead of the publisher paying for attention, and the software never has to guess how much detail a page should ship with.

Reading less, on purpose

There is a subtler benefit that only shows up after a few weeks of use. A stretched page tells you what you skipped. When you shrink a section and leave it shrunk, you have made a small, visible decision about what you do not need, and the shape of the page remembers it. Most reading tools hide that decision; they scroll past it at speed, or collapse it into a summary you never reopen.

Select any paragraph on this page, press Alt+S, and the extension replaces it with a short version. Dotted phrases grow the detail back where it stands; the small arrow at the end puts the original text back.
